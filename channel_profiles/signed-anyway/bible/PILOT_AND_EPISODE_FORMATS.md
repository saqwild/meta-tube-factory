# Story Architecture and Episode Formats

## Purpose of this document

This file defines how a **Signed Anyway** story is conceived, tested and shaped before scriptwriting. It governs the full channel rather than prescribing the plot of one episode.

The tribunal is the repeatable presentation frame. The underlying story may be a marriage betrayal, corporate collapse, near-future decision or crisis response, but every episode must deliver the same audience promise:

> Someone made a consequential choice involving money, power or duty. The choice helped someone, harmed someone else, and left enough moral uncertainty for the viewer to decide the verdict.

The pilot appears near the end as one worked example, not as the universal plot.

## The universal story engine

Every long-form episode follows this causal spine:

**Trust or duty → irreversible decision → apparent benefit → concealed transfer of risk → human consequence → contested evidence → responsibility-changing reversal → viewer verdict**

The decision does not have to be a literal signature. It may be an approval, omission, transfer, override, guarantee, vote, algorithmic instruction or refusal to act.

### One-sentence premise formula

> When **[decision-maker]** secretly **[irreversible choice]** to **[protect a person, institution or principle]**, **[another person/group]** absorbs **[hidden cost]**—but **[later revelation]** makes responsibility harder to assign.

**Corporate example:** When a CFO falsifies a bank certificate to preserve payroll, employees unknowingly absorb the collapse risk—but a hidden rescue offer reveals that the board engineered the emergency.

**Personal example:** When a husband mortgages the family home to keep his sister’s business alive, his wife carries a debt she never accepted—but records show she previously concealed the inheritance that could have prevented the loan.

**Future example:** When a city AI freezes thousands of accounts to stop a synthetic-bank run, innocent residents lose access to medicine—but the emergency model was activated after executives suppressed an accurate human warning.

## The eight required plot components

| Component | Development question | What good execution looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Trust contract | Who owed honesty, consent, loyalty or professional duty to whom? | A relationship the audience understands immediately: spouses, executive/workers, bank/depositor, system/citizen |
| Irreversible decision | What did someone sign, approve, conceal, transfer, override or refuse? | A specific choice made by an identifiable person—not a vague institutional failure |
| Apparent necessity | Why could a reasonable person make this choice? | Time pressure, competing duties, incomplete information or credible fear of a worse outcome |
| Immediate benefit | Who was protected first, and what visible loss was avoided? | Payroll clears, treatment continues, a family remains housed or panic is temporarily contained |
| Hidden cost transfer | Who carried risk without informed consent? | The moral center of the episode; harm is shifted rather than eliminated |
| Material consequence | What measurable and human damage followed? | Money plus a face: lost pension and delayed retirement, frozen funds and missed medicine |
| Contested evidence | Which document, timeline, recording or contradiction tests each account? | Evidence changes what the characters can credibly claim; it is not decorative exposition |
| Responsibility reversal | What fact redistributes blame without erasing the original choice? | The decision-maker remains accountable, but another party’s knowledge, concealment or benefit changes the verdict |

### Reversal rule

The reversal must **reframe responsibility**, not replace one cartoon villain with another. It should make the viewer reconsider the meaning of earlier evidence while preserving the agency of the person who made the central choice.

Good reversal:

> The CFO lied, but the board deliberately hid the rescue offer that would have made the lie unnecessary.

Weak reversal:

> The CFO was innocent because an unknown hacker forged everything.

The good version complicates a choice. The weak version cancels the story.

## Character functions within a case

A finished episode normally uses three to five speaking characters. These are **story functions**, not permanently assigned cast members.

| Function | Dramatic job | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Decision-maker | Made or authorized the central choice | What did you know, choose and expect? |
| Moral claimant | Represents the person or group that absorbed the cost | Why was this risk not yours to assign? |
| Beneficiary, enabler or second witness | Complicates causation and exposes another duty | Who benefited, assisted, remained silent or profited? |
| Examiner | Forces claims into specific choices and consequences | What alternative did you reject? |
| Evidence specialist | Reconstructs mechanism, money flow or timeline | What does the record prove—and what can it not prove? |

One story character may serve two functions. For example, the harmed spouse may also be the person whose earlier concealment creates the reversal. Evelyn Shaw and Noah Vale can provide the examiner and evidence functions, but an episode may reduce one anchor’s presence if the drama is stronger without both.

### Emotional ownership

Every episode must identify one **emotional owner**: the person whose loss, fear or impossible duty gives the evidence human meaning. This person need not be legally right or morally innocent.

Without an emotional owner, the episode risks becoming an illustrated accounting problem.

## Three-act episode architecture

The timing below is a flexible target for an 8–11 minute episode, with 9:30 as the production target. Story causality takes priority over hitting an exact timestamp. The governing formula is **story out = final runtime minus 20 seconds**; the final 20 seconds carry the viewer verdict and YouTube end-screen elements. In the target cut, the dramatic story therefore ends at 9:10 and the verdict/end screen runs from 9:10–9:30.

| Act | Approx. time | Story work |
|---|---:|---|
| Act I — Judgment begins | 0:00–2:15 | Show the consequence, identify the decision and establish the decision-maker’s strongest defense |
| Act II — The cost changes hands | 2:15–6:30 | Test testimony, reconstruct the mechanism, reveal who absorbed the risk and introduce the responsibility-changing fact |
| Act III — No clean exit | 6:30–9:10 | Re-examine choices after the reversal and give each side its strongest closing argument |
| Verdict/end screen | 9:10–9:30 | Present two defensible options while one Best-for-viewer element and Subscribe remain clear of the verdict copy |

### Default beat map

| Target window | Beat | Required outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Consequence cold open | A specific loss and unanswered question |
| 0:20–1:00 | Charge | Decision, scale, affected party and disputed duty are clear |
| 1:00–2:30 | Strongest defense | The audience can understand why the decision appeared necessary |
| 2:30–4:00 | First contradiction | Testimony and record cannot both be completely true |
| 4:00–5:30 | Evidence reconstruction | Show how benefit and risk moved through the system |
| 5:15–6:30 | Responsibility reversal | Earlier evidence gains a different moral meaning |
| 5:15–6:30 natural break | Manual mid-roll opportunity | Use only after a completed evidence/reversal beat; never interrupt a sentence, action or payoff |
| 6:30–8:20 | Choice under pressure | Characters confront alternatives, consent, knowledge and benefit |
| 8:20–9:10 | Opposing closings | Two credible interpretations, neither treated as foolish |
| Final 20 sec | Viewer verdict and end screen | Exactly two clear options, Best for viewer and Subscribe; no creator verdict |

This beat map is a starting structure. Do not stretch weak testimony merely to fill a window.

## Narrative arrangements

The tribunal remains recognizable, but the route through the evidence should vary.

| Arrangement | Best used when | Opening method | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consequence-first hearing | Default for clear central decisions | Damage first, then examiner states the charge | Can become formulaic if every episode uses identical coverage |
| Dual testimony | A relationship contains two credible versions of the same betrayal | Matching statements that contradict each other | Repetition unless each account adds new facts |
| Countdown reconstruction | The decision occurred during a short crisis window | Clock, alerts and escalating constraints | Action can overpower the moral choice |
| Evidence-led mystery | The responsible decision-maker is initially uncertain | A document, transfer or recording becomes the hook | Must not hide basic information unfairly |
| Aftermath reconstruction | The consequence is visually stronger than the hearing | Rebuild the incident, then test responsibility | Reconstruction can feel like a separate documentary |
| Future protocol hearing | A system or AI executed the harm | Rule output first, human authorization later | Blaming “the algorithm” instead of human choices |

No single arrangement should account for more than half of the first ten episodes.

## Case families

The categories determine the source of the moral pressure; they do not change the channel promise.

| Main category | Planning share | Recurring trust relationship | Typical hidden cost | Example premise |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Money Between Us | 40% | Spouses, siblings, parent/child, close partners | Debt, inheritance, housing, retirement or sacrificed opportunity | A wife secretly empties a retirement account to keep her husband out of prison, then learns he had already rejected a legal settlement |
| Corporate Reckonings | 40% | Executive/workers, board/shareholders, company/customer, official/public | Jobs, pensions, deposits, safety, public loss or displaced crisis damage | A CFO lies to preserve payroll while a board suppresses the rescue offer that would remove them |
| Future Verdicts | 20% | Citizen/system, patient/provider, worker/automation owner | Access, privacy, livelihood, autonomy or unequal risk | A medical AI trades one patient’s treatment slot to save five statistically stronger candidates |

The financial mechanism should remain understandable in plain language. The episode is about the choice and its human cost—not displaying technical sophistication. Crisis Reconstruction is a cross-pillar visual device rather than a fourth case family. When a crisis dominates a corporate case, label it **Corporate Reckonings — Event Special**; do not create a separate crisis playlist.

## Reusable plot engines

These engines create variety without changing the brand.

| Plot engine | Core conflict | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The noble lie | Protection now versus consent and damage later | A founder falsifies solvency to make one final payroll |
| The transferred loss | A crisis is solved by assigning the cost to an unseen group | A bank rescues depositors by voiding small-business credit lines |
| The unauthorized sacrifice | One person gambles with something jointly owned | A spouse guarantees a sibling’s loan with the family home |
| The delayed warning | Disclosure now causes panic; silence increases later harm | A risk officer delays a breach announcement during acquisition talks |
| The competing duty | A character cannot satisfy two legitimate obligations | A trustee protects pension liquidity by abandoning a town’s largest employer |
| The delegated conscience | A person relies on a model, protocol or vote to escape responsibility | An insurer lets an AI deny emergency coverage without reviewing edge cases |
| The mutual concealment | Both sides hid different facts, but only one triggered the collapse | Each spouse concealed assets for a different claimed reason |
| The chosen scapegoat | An institution preserves public trust by concentrating blame | A junior analyst accepts responsibility for a board-approved risk model |

Avoid using the same plot engine, central relationship and reversal type in consecutive long-form episodes.

## Worked story examples

### Example A — Money Between Us

**Working title:** He Used Their Home to Save His Sister

- **Trust contract:** Spouses jointly own their home and promised that neither would borrow against it alone.
- **Decision:** The husband uses an outdated power of attorney to guarantee his sister’s business loan.
- **Apparent necessity:** Without the guarantee, the business closes that morning and twelve employees lose their final wages.
- **Hidden cost:** His wife’s home and financial independence become collateral without consent.
- **Consequence:** The sister defaults; foreclosure proceedings begin.
- **Reversal:** The wife had hidden an inheritance in a separate account because she planned to leave the marriage. It could have covered the shortfall, but the husband did not know it existed.
- **Verdict A:** His emergency motive does not excuse assigning another person’s home without consent.
- **Verdict B:** His betrayal remains real, but both spouses’ concealment created the disaster and responsibility is shared.

### Example B — Corporate Reckonings

**Working title:** He Saved 2,000 Jobs by Lying to the Bank

- **Trust contract:** A CFO owes truthful certification to the lender and stewardship to employees.
- **Decision:** He signs a false covenant certificate so the bank will not freeze the credit line.
- **Apparent necessity:** Payroll clears and 2,000 workers remain employed for ninety-one days.
- **Hidden cost:** Workers continue laboring while severance and pension contributions become less recoverable.
- **Consequence:** The rescue fails; employees lose jobs, severance and part of a pension payment.
- **Reversal:** The board concealed a legitimate rescue offer because accepting it would remove them; the auditor also delayed reporting for forty-eight hours.
- **Verdict A:** The CFO made a defensible emergency choice inside a crisis engineered by the board.
- **Verdict B:** Whatever the board did, he knowingly made workers and the bank finance a gamble they never accepted.

### Example C — Future Verdicts

**Working title:** The AI Froze 40,000 Accounts to Stop a Bank Run

- **Trust contract:** A public banking system promises access to lawful funds and protection from systemic collapse.
- **Decision:** The emergency director authorizes an AI to freeze accounts predicted to spread withdrawal panic.
- **Apparent necessity:** The freeze stops a cascading bank failure within seventeen minutes.
- **Hidden cost:** Thousands of innocent account holders cannot buy medicine, make payroll or leave unsafe situations.
- **Consequence:** One small employer collapses and preventable medical harm occurs.
- **Reversal:** Human analysts had correctly identified the manipulation campaign six hours earlier, but executives suppressed their warning to protect a pending merger.
- **Verdict A:** The director used the only remaining tool capable of stopping mass financial collapse.
- **Verdict B:** Emergency scale does not justify punishment by prediction, especially after institutional concealment created the deadline.

### Example D — Corporate Reckonings: Event Special

**Working title:** She Saved the Hospital by Flooding 600 Homes

- **Trust contract:** A city engineer must protect the whole district under an emergency flood plan.
- **Decision:** She opens a diversion barrier that saves the hospital but redirects water into a residential neighborhood.
- **Apparent necessity:** The hospital has intensive-care patients who cannot be evacuated before the levee fails.
- **Hidden cost:** Six hundred households receive the flood without adequate warning.
- **Consequence:** The hospital survives; homes and family businesses are destroyed.
- **Reversal:** The neighborhood was designated as the emergency flood basin years earlier, but officials suppressed the notice to preserve property values and tax revenue.
- **Verdict A:** The engineer made the least-worst choice available during a life-threatening emergency.
- **Verdict B:** She executed an unjust plan and treated one community as expendable without informed consent.

## Premise selection gate

Score each proposed episode from 0–2 on every dimension:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision clarity | No identifiable choice | Choice exists but is diffuse | One specific irreversible choice |
| Human consequence | Abstract or technical | Understandable but distant | Specific, measurable and emotionally embodied |
| Credible defense | Decision is plainly foolish or malicious | Thin justification | A reasonable viewer could initially agree |
| Hidden cost | No transfer of risk | Cost is predictable | Cost creates a meaningful consent or duty conflict |
| Evidence potential | Mostly verbal explanation | One usable proof point | Several visual documents, timelines or contradictions |
| Reversal quality | Cancels the story | Adds information | Redistributes responsibility and reinterprets earlier evidence |
| Verdict balance | One obvious answer | Second option requires effort | Two concise positions can attract fair-minded support |
| Production fitness | Requires many locations/crowds/actions | Achievable with compromises | Works with 3–5 speakers, recurring sets and controlled reconstructions |

**Greenlight threshold:** at least 13/16, with no zero in decision clarity, credible defense, reversal quality or verdict balance.

## Plot guardrails

- Do not begin with a moral topic and attach arbitrary characters; begin with a consequential choice under pressure.
- Do not make poverty, ethnicity, nationality, disability, religion or accent shorthand for guilt or innocence.
- Do not confuse secrecy with complexity. A withheld fact is useful only if it changes interpretation.
- Do not make the harmed party morally perfect merely to manufacture sympathy.
- Do not rely on an unsupported final-minute twist.
- Do not let an AI, board, market or institution act as an abstract villain; identify the humans who authorized, benefited, ignored or refused.
- Do not reproduce a recognizable real case with fictional names.
- Do not teach an actionable fraud method. Show consequences and responsibility, not an operational tutorial.
- Do not issue an official legal conclusion. The ending asks for moral judgment between two defensible interpretations.

## Pilot role and validation

The first episode, **He Saved 2,000 Jobs by Lying to the Bank**, is a test of the flagship format—not the template for every later plot. It tests whether viewers:

- understand the decision and scale within the first 45 seconds;
- remain through the evidence reconstruction and responsibility reversal;
- argue for both verdict positions in comments;
- recognize the format when the next episode changes category and relationship;
- accept the recurring cast without confusing performer identity with episode role;
- respond to the story as crafted drama rather than merely “AI-generated video.”

Review the pilot after 48 hours, 7 days and 28 days using the measurements in `EPISODE_SPEC.md`. Do not rewrite the overall story system around one upload’s early performance.
