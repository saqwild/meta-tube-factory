The name works because every episode turns on an intentional decision—a signature, approval, concealment or transfer—that seemed defensible until its cost became visible. It is broad enough for marriage, inheritance, corporate and future cases.

## Audience persona: The Ethical Investigator

| Attribute | Profile |
|---|---|
| Age | Primary 25–44; secondary 18–24 and 45–54 |
| Interests | Money psychology, corporate scandals, relationship betrayal, legal/ethical dilemmas, near-future fiction |
| Pain points | Generic AI stories, one-dimensional villains, slow exposition, factual claims without evidence |
| Goal | Experience a smart story, test personal judgment, and debate the decision with others |
| Viewing triggers | A specific amount, an irreversible choice, a hidden relationship, a ticking clock, a credible “what would you do?” |
| Return trigger | Recurring examiner, familiar evidence-room grammar and a new verdict each week |

## Voice rules

- Write with controlled tension and plain language.
- Let characters argue motives; do not explain morality for them.
- Use financial detail as evidence, not a lecture.
- End on a decision the viewer can answer in one comment.
- Never call the work “true,” “based on a true story,” or “documentary” unless a separately approved factual episode meets a higher sourcing standard.

Use faceless channel branding but expressive fictional faces in thumbnails. The brass signature mark remains the avatar. Cinematic imagery should feel authored and editorial, not glossy corporate stock.

## Thumbnail rules

1. Use a heavily darkened, episode-specific set plate grounded in Ink navy; the plate changes with the case.
2. Place one expressive fictional face on the right, occupying 40–50% of the frame, unless a decisive object is demonstrably stronger.
3. Place zero to three words on the left third in Paper-colored Source Serif 4. If quotation marks are used, the words must be an exact line spoken or shown in the episode.
4. Place one story-critical amount or number at bottom-left in Aged-brass IBM Plex Mono. It must be accurate within the fictional case and appear on screen by 0:10.
5. Use one thin fixed brass hairline and, when useful, a small fictional sector/company silhouette—never a real logo.
6. Oxblood may occupy no more than 3% of the frame; never use a large red balance, panel or glow.
7. Generate a 3840×2160 16:9 master and compress the upload below 2 MB when practical. Keep critical content inside the central 80% and verify at 10% scale.
8. Reserve the lower-right 25% width × 18% height for the YouTube duration/interface overlay. Keep the bottom-left number at least 10% inward from the left and bottom edges.
9. Ban arrows, circles, red rings, shocked-face-plus-explosion compositions, episode numbers, more than three words, fake news lower-thirds and unreadable generated documents.

Each dialogue-driven fictional story reconstructs the evidence behind a hidden financial decision—then asks you to decide who crossed the line.

## Title grammar

- **[Person] + [hidden act] + [specific consequence]**
- **The [role] had [time limit] to explain [specific loss]**
- **[Technology/decision] did [harm]. Who is responsible?**

Lead with the event, not “The Moral Ledger,” a season number or an AI-production label.
